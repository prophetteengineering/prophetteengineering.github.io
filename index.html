Bell nozzle length trade study

The team's next engine is moving from a conical nozzle to a bell. I compared five bell lengths in RPA and CFD to find where the trade between performance and length lands, and the team adopted the result.

Role
Lead analyst, independent study
Timeline
September 2026
Tools
RPA, Onshape, Ansys Discovery, ANSYS Fluent
Deliverable
43-page technical report (v1.1)

The problem

The engine is a 2,000 lbf LOX/RP-1 design at 355 psia. Bell length is one of the first geometry decisions on a bell nozzle. A longer bell turns the flow straighter and gains specific impulse, but it costs length, mass and cooled wall area. I was asked to find where that trade lands.

Approach

  • Generated Rao parabolic contours for 60, 70, 80, 90 and 100% bell lengths in RPA at the same chamber conditions (O/F 1.7) and expansion ratio (Ae/At = 3.83).
  • Built each flow domain in Onshape and Ansys Discovery, matching RPA's wall coordinates within 0.01 mm.
  • Ran 2D axisymmetric CFD in Fluent: pressure-based coupled solver, k-ω SST, 0.5 mm quad mesh with 15 inflation layers (wall y+ ≤ 7.3).
  • Computed thrust, mass flow and Isp from exit-plane integrals, and checked every case for mass balance (under 0.01% imbalance) and flat monitors.

Results

Bell length60%70%80%90%100%
Sea-level Isp, CFD (s)236.0240.8243.9246.0247.2
  • Diminishing returns. The gain per 10% step falls from about 4.7 s (60 to 70%) to about 1.3 s (90 to 100%), while each step adds the same 11 mm or so of length.
  • No separation risk. The lowest wall pressure, at the lip, is 134 to 139 kPa in every case. The Summerfield separation threshold at sea level is roughly 35 to 40 kPa.
  • Checked against RPA. After applying RPA's combustion efficiency, CFD lands within about 1% of RPA on mass flow, thrust coefficient and delivered Isp. All five cases produce 1,933 to 2,039 lbf, consistent with the 2,000 lbf target.
  • Where the methods disagree. RPA's delivered Isp falls slightly with length while CFD rises 4.8%. RPA's 1-D loss models appear to weight wall friction over divergence loss. The CFD resolves the flow turning directly and matches classical nozzle theory.

Inside the 80% case

The converged 80% case passes 3.7317 kg/s in and out with less than 0.01% imbalance, produces 8,927 N of sea-level thrust and leaves the nozzle at Mach 2.59 on average. I first ran it with the density-based solver, which left about a 5% mass imbalance in the slow chamber flow. Switching to the pressure-based coupled solver fixed it, and that switch is the step in the residual history.

Recommendation: the 80% bell

The 80% bell keeps 98.7% of the full bell's Isp (243.9 s against 247.2 s) while being about 21 mm shorter, with a large separation margin. A 90% bell is a reasonable alternative if 2 s more Isp is worth 11 mm of extra cooled wall once the cooling approach and material are chosen. The team adopted the 80% bell.

Follow-on: the engine design point

After the study I completed the O/F 1.7 table of the team's RPA sweep (11 cases over Pc/Pe 21 to 31) and reviewed the propulsion lead's 121-point sweep of O/F against pressure ratio. Apogee varied only about 1.6% across that sweep, so I argued for choosing the point on thermal grounds. I proposed O/F 2.0 with Pc/Pe 26, which matches exit pressure to ambient at the launch site, and the team adopted it as the design point.

What went wrong, and what I learned

  • Check that inputs actually change outputs. My first five RPA runs all produced the same near-conical shape. I had set bell length only in the nozzle-efficiency panel, not in the contour geometry. I caught it by measuring wall angles in the exported DXFs and fixed it before running any CFD.
  • Convergence is more than residuals. The density-based solver left about a 5% mass imbalance in the slow chamber flow, so I switched to pressure-based coupled. Starting the chamber nearly at rest stopped the early pressure sloshing.
  • Small geometry errors matter. A 0.49 mm axis offset in the 70% case enlarged its effective throat and inflated mass flow by 3.4%. I corrected it and re-ran the case.
  • Still open. A mesh-independence study on the 80% case.

Nozzle heat flux and wall thermal analysis

Can an uncooled heat-sink chamber survive a ten-second burn? I wrote a Python thermal model to find out, checked it against a 2D Ansys model, and then worked out how much film cooling the wall needs.

Role
Analyst. I wrote the physics modules and ran the Ansys model.
Timeline
October 2026 to present
Tools
Python (NumPy, SciPy, Matplotlib), Ansys Transient Thermal, SpaceClaim
Status
Heat flux, heat-sink check and first film-cooling pass done

The problem

Once the design point moved to O/F 2.0 and Pc/Pe 26, the propulsion lead asked me to verify the new chamber geometry and then estimate chamber and nozzle heat flux. The simplest chamber to build is a thick uncooled wall that soaks up heat for the length of the burn. The question was whether that could work for about 10 seconds.

Approach

  • Geometry check. Rebuilt the contour from the team's RPA DXF export and checked it against the design targets to within 0.25%: throat diameter 57.33 mm, contraction ratio 6.00, expansion ratio 4.31, bell angles 22.0° and 14.0°, 93.2 mm from throat to exit.
  • Flow. Wrote an isentropic area-Mach solver using a bracketed root finder (scipy.optimize.brentq) with subsonic and supersonic branch selection. Chamber Mach number is 0.0996.
  • Gas-side heat transfer. Implemented the Bartz correlation with the σ boundary-layer correction along the contour.
  • Wall response. Wrote a 1-D explicit finite-difference transient conduction solver for the throat wall, with a convection boundary on the hot side, an insulated outer wall and a 300 K start. Ran copper, 304 stainless and Inconel 718 at 6, 10 and 20 mm.

Results

Peak heat flux is 56.9 MW/m² at the throat (adiabatic wall temperature 3,191 K, wall at 850 K), about five times the chamber value.

A pure heat-sink wall does not survive. At the throat, a 10 mm copper wall reaches its 850 K limit in 0.090 s and its melting point at 0.436 s. 304 stainless reaches melting at 0.055 s and Inconel 718 at 0.024 s. None of them comes close to a 10 s burn.

Check against Ansys

To check the 1-D model I built a 2D axisymmetric Ansys Transient Thermal model of the chamber and nozzle wall: copper alloy, 10 mm wall, 1 mm elements, 300 K start, with the Bartz results applied as 12 convection loads along the inner wall.

Copper throat wall, 10 mm1-D Python2D Ansys
Time to reach the 850 K limit0.09 s0.12 s
Time to reach copper's melting point (1,358 K)0.44 s0.53 s

The 2D model heats a little more slowly, but both give the same answer to the design question: the wall is past its limit in about a tenth of a second.

Getting the Ansys model to mesh took two fixes: the polyline DXF imported as hundreds of 1 mm edges, which I replaced with a spline-based DXF, and a millimeter-versus-meter import error in SpaceClaim.

How much film cooling it needs

I extended the wall solver with a film-effectiveness model, Taw,eff = Taw − η(Taw − Tcoolant), and swept η for stainless and Inconel at the throat (10 mm wall, 300 K coolant).

  • The walls need η of about 0.72 to 0.81 at the throat, depending on the allowable temperature.
  • There is a cliff between 0.70 and 0.75. At 0.70, stainless passes 1,100 K at 5.6 s and Inconel at 3.6 s. At 0.75, neither does within the burn.
  • Staying under 850 K for the full 10 s takes η of about 0.8.

Chamber sizing check

Numerical integration of the contour gives a chamber volume of 2.599 L, L* of 1,007 mm (the usual LOX/RP-1 range is 1.0 to 1.3 m) and a residence time of 1.38 ms. The design contraction ratio of 6.00 is above the 4.06 from the Humble correlation, and the Rayleigh stagnation-pressure loss at chamber Mach 0.0996 is 0.57%.

Limits of the model

  • Bartz with equilibrium gas properties tends to overpredict, so these heat transfer coefficients are an upper bound.
  • The Ansys check covers the copper case only so far.
  • The results raised questions I took back to the team: whether the 10 s burn is fixed, how much fuel can go to film cooling, and the injector and alloy choices that follow.

COPV pressurant manifold

A custom manifold that mounts directly on the rocket's high-pressure nitrogen tank and feeds every downstream line. I took it from interface definition through CAD, drawing, hand calculations and FEA, and it is now cleared for the machine shop.

Role
Design lead
Timeline
October 2026 to present
Tools
SolidWorks, Onshape, Ansys Mechanical
Deliverables
Drawing BLT-FL-001 Rev A, manufacturing plan, stress analysis report

The problem

The vehicle's COPV fed its downstream lines through a tee fitting and loose tubing. The fluids lead assigned me to replace that with one custom manifold mounted directly to the COPV. It sits upstream of regulation in the GN2 pressurant system, so the body sees full storage pressure, and every downstream tubing run on the vehicle depended on it being defined on time.

First version: four ports, delivered October 4

I used the P&ID and the team's work breakdown to confirm the outlets, and measured the COPV boss from the team's Onshape model instead of relying on verbal specs: 7/8-14 UNF, an SAE J1926 -10 O-ring boss. I later removed a fitting from the actual COPV to confirm the O-ring boss connection on hardware.

I started with machined 37° flare seats, but the revolve features were costing time with two days left. I moved to a simpler cross-drilled layout, which is also easier to machine.

Rev A: six ports, after the design review

The October 12 review added ports for a relief valve and a pressure transducer, and I moved every port to an O-ring boss. After talking with the university machine shop technician I resized the bores to standard 5/8 in and 3/8 in drills so the part can be made on a manual mill.

Stress analysis

Before the part goes to the shop it has to be shown to hold 4,000 psi and the planned 6,000 psi proof. The block is only 1.40 in across with six ports cut into it, so the walls between the ports and the places where drilled holes meet were the concern.

Hand calculation

I treated each port as a thick-walled cylinder (Lamé), with the outer radius taken as the distance from the port center to the nearest outside face. For the threaded ports I used half the thread major diameter as the inner radius, which makes the wall thinner than it really is, so the result is conservative.

LocationVon Mises at 4,000 psiSafety factor at 4,000 psiAt 6,000 psi
-10 port11,369 psi3.082.05
-6 ports8,262 psi4.242.82
Center passage8,653 psi4.042.70

The -10 port governs because it has the largest bore and the thinnest wall. A Faupel burst estimate for it gives about 22,200 psi, roughly 5.5 times MEOP. None of these formulas account for holes intersecting each other, and that is the reason for the finite element model.

Finite element model

Linear static model in Ansys Mechanical, built from the SolidWorks STEP export, with quadratic tetrahedra (90,092 nodes, 52,129 elements). The student license caps a model at 128,000 nodes, so the mesh keeps a coarse background and refines to 0.008 in only along the intersection where the stress peaks.

Loads. 4,000 psi on every wetted face. Pressure alone is not in equilibrium, because each open port in the model is where a fitting would sit. So I added the thrust each fitting puts on its threads, sized from the outer diameter of the port's entry chamfer: 2,616 lbf at the -10 port and 1,179 lbf at each -6 port.

Supports. Since the loads balance on their own, the supports only need to stop rigid-body motion. One pinned vertex removes the three translations, and a deformable remote displacement on one outer face removes the three rotations. I did not fix a whole face, because that would over-constrain the block and create stress that does not exist in the real part.

Checking the model before trusting it

  • Equilibrium. The support reactions should be close to zero, and they are: 0.28 lbf, about 0.01% of the COPV thrust force. This check caught a real setup error. My first solve returned a 198 lbf reaction, because I had calculated the thrust forces from the bore diameters while the pressure also acted on the entry chamfers. Recalculating from the chamfer diameters fixed it.
  • Agreement with the hand calculation. Halfway down the -10 bore, away from any intersection, the model reads 10,754 psi against 10,447 psi from Lamé at the same radius. That is within 3%.
  • Mesh convergence. Four meshes with smaller and smaller elements at the hot spot. Every refinement changed the peak by less than 1%, and the peak levels off instead of climbing, so it is a real stress concentration and not a singularity at a sharp edge.

At 4,000 psi the manifold does not yield. The port walls clear the 1.5 requirement with a wide margin: 3.08 by hand and 3.25 in the model. The local peak where the drilled holes cross is a different story. The stress concentration there is about 2.4, which gives a safety factor of 1.38, roughly 8% short of 1.5. Whether the requirement applies to that local peak or to the general wall stress has not been settled with the fluids lead, so the report gives both.

At the 6,000 psi proof, a small amount of local yielding is predicted at the same intersections, starting at about 5,500 psi. The number is an upper bound: the model leaves the crossing edges sharp, real 6061-T6 stock is usually stronger than the minimum, and a linear model overstates stress once the material yields.

Where it stands

  • Confirming with the fluids lead how the 1.5 factor applies, and whether local yielding at proof is acceptable.
  • Modeling the 0.015 in deburr radius the drawing calls for at the port intersections, to see how much it lowers the peak. The finer mesh runs into the student license node limit, so this case is still in progress.
  • If more margin is needed, the options are 7075-T6 (a safety factor near 2.9 at the same peak) or a lower proof pressure. A lower proof would also suit the COPV fitting, which is rated to 5,000 psi.
  • Still to do on the analysis: a hand check of thread shear and a nonlinear case at proof pressure.
  • The machine shop request is approved. The plan is a 6061 practice part, then the flight part, on a manual mill, followed by a hydrostatic proof test with dimensional checks before and after.

Read the stress analysis report

What I learned

  • Interfaces first. Pinning down port count, thread specs and pressure basis from the P&ID and existing CAD made the modeling itself fast.
  • Simplify to ship. When the first approach threatened a deadline the whole fluid system depended on, a simpler design that met the requirements was the right call.
  • Intersections govern. The port walls passed by hand with a factor above 3. The crossing bores are what set the margin, and only the FEA showed it.
  • Don't trust exports to carry intent. Cosmetic thread callouts don't reliably survive the SolidWorks-to-Onshape import, so I sent thread sizes to the fluids lead directly.

The rocket's fluid system is being rebuilt for a new airframe. I calculated the loss coefficient of every fitting on the vehicle, and I'm co-leading the upper fluids CAD with the fluids lead.

Fitting loss coefficients

The team needed flow resistance coefficients for every fitting on the Theseus 2 fluids component list. I calculated them with Crane TP-410, working out fitting orientation and flow direction from the lower, middle and upper fluids STEP assemblies, and delivered a formula-driven workbook with a calculations document.

Along the way I found three errors in the worked example we had been given: a roughness typo, viscosity in the wrong units, and an operator error in the Swamee-Jain equation. Together they had produced K = 1.09 for a LOX elbow. I then compared methods for the same elbow.

The workbook covers all 93 lines of the component list. Seventy-one get a K value, and tanks, transducers, solenoids and regulators are marked as needing supplier data. A 17-page calculation document goes with it, with the Crane formula and a fully worked example for each fitting type. For the two check valves I sourced the supplier flow coefficients (Cv 0.67 and 0.21) and converted them to K.

Operating conditions and COPV fill pressure

For the Fluids CDR a teammate and I worked out the vehicle's operating conditions and checked them against the Theseus 1 design review: 23.77 lb of LOX and 16.73 lb of kerosene for an 8.87 s burn, set by keeping 116 in³ of ullage in each tank, and a total impulse of 7,362 lbf·s at the LR-101's 830 lbf.

The nitrogen side needed a closer look. The usual minimum COPV pressure comes from Boyle's law, which assumes the gas stays at room temperature. An 8.87 s blowdown is closer to adiabatic, so the gas cools and less of it comes out before the bottle reaches its 800 psi floor. Treating the blowdown as adiabatic raises the minimum fill from 2,352 psi to about 2,780 psi, and to about 3,680 psi once the LOX tank chills the nitrogen and more gas is needed to hold pressure.

Valve testing and test operations

My first work on the team was hands-on: qualifying the valves that fly on the rocket, and running the control GUI during checkouts. It is also where I caught a timing problem with the igniters before a static fire.

Role
Test engineer
Timeline
2023
Hardware
5 solenoid valves, 3 ball valves, flight tubing
Methods
Decay test, hydrostatic test, leak check, water flow

Valve testing

  • Decay-tested 8 valves (5 solenoid, 3 ball; flight units plus spares) at 100 psi.
  • Logged pressure through the control GUI, reduced the data in Excel, and flagged the 3 valves that failed.
  • Hydrostatically tested the valves, ran leak checks and water flow tests, flared tubing, and sealed NPT threads with PTFE tape.
  • Operated the control GUI and worked stand-side during valve click checks.

The igniter timing finding

In lab testing ahead of a static fire I found that the igniters fired before the control GUI indicated, which pointed to unreliable timing code. I reported it before the test and raised it again during the test. Early ignition was one of several issues that damaged the test stand.

I proposed an overhaul of the ground-support GUI to catch that class of problem earlier. The team has since redesigned the GUI and the stand, and went on to a successful launch.

What it does

  • Simulates powered ascent, coast, apogee, descent and landing under thrust, gravity and aerodynamic drag.
  • Advances the state with a fixed time step using semi-implicit Euler integration.
  • Split into modules for constants, environment, forces, the rocket and the simulation engine, so each part can be replaced as the model grows.
  • Prints a flight summary, saves altitude, velocity and acceleration plots, and handles a failed-launch case.
  • Documented in a 23-page technical report in the repository.

About

Demetrius Prophette

Aerospace Engineering student at Cal State Long Beach, working toward a career in propulsion and fluid systems test engineering.

I've wanted to work on spacecraft for a long time, and rocketry is how I've turned that into engineering work. I joined Beach Launch Team, CSULB's liquid rocketry team, in 2023. I started on the test side, qualifying valves and running the control GUI, and I now work across combustion devices and fluids.

This year that has meant a nozzle trade study the team adopted, a thermal model of the next engine's chamber wall, and a high-pressure manifold for the Theseus 2 rocket that I've taken from a blank part file to an approved machine shop request.

I'm aiming at test engineering in propulsion and fluid systems. Alongside the analysis work I'm building up hands-on shop experience, starting with machining the manifold myself. I'm looking for a Summer 2027 internship in propulsion or fluid systems, in test or design.

Outside the team I serve as Co-Director of Finance for the Associated Engineering Student Body and I'm a member of the National Society of Black Engineers.
